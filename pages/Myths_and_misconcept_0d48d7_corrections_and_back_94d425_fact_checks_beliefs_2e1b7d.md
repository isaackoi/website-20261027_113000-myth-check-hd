## Introduction

Fact-checks often make political beliefs more accurate without changing how people vote. The evidence for this pattern is reasonably strong: experiments conducted during elections have repeatedly found that participants accept at least part of a correction while retaining much the same view of the candidate or policy involved.[1][2][3] That does not show that facts are powerless. It shows that a vote is not a quiz answer.

Voters weigh party loyalty, values, group identity, economic interests, trust, candidate character and comparisons with the alternative. Correcting one campaign claim changes only one input into that larger judgement. Effects on votes are possible, especially when corrected information is important, credible and previously unknown, but they are generally smaller and less consistent than effects on factual accuracy. Most importantly, unchanged support is not a backfire effect unless the correction actually increases belief in the false claim.

## The verdict in practical terms

The best-supported assessment is that fact-checking is primarily an accuracy intervention, not a dependable vote-switching device. It can help citizens distinguish substantiated claims from false ones, but the research does not justify promising that a successful correction will alter candidate support, policy preferences or election results.

Three qualifications keep that conclusion from becoming another myth.

First, an average effect is not a universal rule. Some corrections do affect candidate evaluations or voting intentions, particularly when the disputed fact is central to the decision.[4][5] Secondly, most studies measure immediate survey responses rather than verified votes cast weeks or months later. That makes the evidence about beliefs much stronger than the evidence about final electoral behaviour. Thirdly, a small average voting effect could still matter in a close election, even when it is difficult to detect in an individual experiment.

The practical distinction is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- **Correction succeeds:** belief in the false claim decreases.
- **Correction has no attitudinal spillover:** candidate or policy support remains similar.
- **Correction backfires:** belief in the false claim becomes stronger than it would have been without correction.

</div>

Only the third result supports a factual backfire claim. Political disappointment with the first two does not change their meaning.

## Experiments test two different outcomes

Researchers can separate factual updating from political persuasion by asking different questions after randomly assigning participants to receive a correction or a control message.

A belief measure might ask whether crime rose, whether unemployment reached a stated level or whether a candidate supported a particular proposal. An attitudinal measure asks how favourably the participant views the candidate, which policy they prefer or how likely they are to vote for someone. Random assignment then reveals whether the correction changed either outcome relative to people who did not see it.

This separation matters because factual questions and political choices have different structures. A factual proposition can often be answered largely by evidence: the official rate was one figure rather than another. Candidate choice is comparative and multi-dimensional. A voter can concede that a preferred candidate used a false statistic yet still prefer that candidate’s policies, party, appointments or perceived character to those of the opponent.

Survey responses also contain complications. Some respondents use factual questions partly to express partisan allegiance. Studies offering small payments for accurate answers have found that partisan gaps sometimes shrink, indicating that at least some apparently false answers represent cheerleading or expressive responding rather than firmly held factual beliefs.[6][7] Fact-checking may therefore change knowledge, willingness to report that knowledge, or both. Neither automatically dictates a vote.

## Trump corrections changed accuracy more than support

A particularly clear demonstration came from Briony Swire and colleagues during Donald Trump’s rise in US politics. Participants evaluated several claims attributed to Trump and then saw corrections. The corrections made assessments of those claims more accurate, including among Trump supporters. Yet the factual updating produced little corresponding movement in voting intentions. Supporters could recognise that particular statements were wrong without withdrawing their broader support.[1]

Brendan Nyhan, Ethan Porter, Jason Reifler and Thomas Wood later examined journalistic fact-checking of Trump during the 2016 presidential campaign. Across three experiments, fact-checks generally improved factual accuracy. The gains were greater among people predisposed to reject Trump, but his supporters also became more accurate. Candidate favourability moved little.[2]

The result is easy to misread. It did not show that Trump supporters were literally impervious to evidence: their factual responses changed in the predicted direction. Nor did it show that corrections strengthened the false claims. It showed that candidate evaluation was buffered from corrections to individual statements.

That buffer can take several forms. A supporter may consider the falsehood unimportant, regard it as ordinary political exaggeration, distrust the fact-checker’s framing or compare it with perceived faults in the opposing candidate. The person may also substitute another justification: “That statistic was wrong, but the underlying problem is real.” Researchers sometimes call this process belief bolstering or rationalisation, but observing stable support alone cannot establish which mechanism operated.

## France showed the same belief-vote gap

A large experiment linked to France’s 2017 presidential election tested false or misleading statistical claims used by Marine Le Pen. Oscar Barrera and colleagues compared exposure to her claims, official statistics and fact-checks that paired the claims with corrections.[3]

The corrections improved factual knowledge. Participants became better able to identify the correct figures. But correcting the numbers did not reliably reverse the broader political consequences of encountering Le Pen’s arguments, including voting intentions. Accurate statistics and electoral preference followed different paths.

This case illustrates why “providing the facts” can be necessary without being sufficient. A disputed statistic may support a campaign narrative—about immigration, unemployment or public services—but it is rarely the narrative’s only support. A voter who accepts the corrected number may retain the same moral priority, perception of social change or distrust of established parties. The correction removes one piece of evidence without necessarily displacing the larger interpretation.

The French study also cautions against evaluating fact-checks by a standard they were not designed to meet. If a correction accurately repairs knowledge, calling it ineffective because it does not determine a vote collapses civic information and electoral persuasion into one task.

## Corrected claims can leave “belief echoes”

Emily Thorson’s research on “belief echoes” offers another explanation for the gap. In her experiments, participants encountered negative political information that was later corrected. Corrections could repair the explicit factual belief while leaving behind a less favourable attitude towards the person targeted by the misinformation.[8]

One route is cognitive: even after a retraction, the initial claim may continue to influence the story people use to explain events. Another is emotional: disgust, anger or suspicion generated by the allegation may outlast memory for its evidential status. A voter may no longer endorse the exact accusation yet retain a vague sense that something troubling surrounds the candidate.

Belief echoes are not inevitable, and their size depends on the message and experimental conditions. They nevertheless show why asking only “Do you now believe the claim?” can miss downstream residue. Conversely, asking only “Do you still dislike the candidate?” can wrongly imply that the correction failed to improve factual understanding.

This is related to the broader continued-influence effect, in which retracted information remains available in memory and reasoning.[9][10] But political attitude stability does not require continued false belief. A person can accurately remember the correction and still decide that it carries little weight in the final choice.

## Why votes are harder to move than factual answers

Several forces dilute the journey from a corrected fact to a changed vote.

**A vote combines many considerations.** A fact-check normally targets one proposition. An electoral decision may reflect dozens of issues plus party identification and views of the opposing candidates. Even a complete reversal on one belief may have little effect on the total balance.

**Party identity acts as an anchor.** Partisanship is not merely a list of policy views; for many citizens it is a social identity. Research on expressive partisanship and affective polarisation shows that attachment to one side and dislike of the other can structure political judgement independently of a single factual dispute.[11][12] A correction must be unusually consequential to overcome that anchor.

**People can update without giving the fact much weight.** Belief and importance are separate. A voter may accept that a figure is inaccurate but regard the underlying topic as secondary to taxation, public services or constitutional questions. Alternatively, the voter may think the error reflects sloppy speaking rather than disqualifying dishonesty.

**Corrections do not erase comparisons.** Elections ask voters to choose between available options, not to decide whether one candidate is flawless. Negative information about a preferred candidate may have little effect if the alternative remains less attractive.

**Attitudes can generate replacement reasons.** When one supporting claim is removed, a person may rely more heavily on other reasons already available. This need not be conscious dishonesty. Political preferences often exist before people are asked to explain them, so a correction may change the explanation offered without changing the preference itself.

**Campaign persuasion is generally difficult.** A large meta-analysis of campaign contact in US general elections found that persuasive effects on candidate choice were close to zero on average, although effects varied by context.[13] Field research on television advertising likewise shows that persuasion can be real but short-lived.[14] Fact-checks operate in the same crowded environment, where competing messages and established preferences limit any single communication’s reach.

These mechanisms do not prove that every stable vote is identity-driven. They explain why belief change should not be expected to pass automatically through every later stage:

`correction → factual updating → issue judgement → candidate evaluation → voting intention → vote cast`

A modest effect can weaken or disappear at each link.

## When fact-checks do affect political choices

The belief-vote gap is common, not absolute. Several experiments show that corrections can influence political evaluations under the right conditions.

Kim Fridkin, Patrick Kenney and Amanda Wintersieck studied fact-checking in the context of negative political advertising. A corrective message could change how participants assessed the attack and, in some conditions, how they evaluated the candidates.[4] Wintersieck’s later experiment with fact-checking during a political debate found that credibility judgements and candidate evaluations responded to information about the accuracy of candidates’ statements.[5]

Corrections may be more likely to travel from belief to choice when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

- the claim concerns a matter voters consider important;
- the new information bears directly on competence, integrity or a central campaign promise;
- the correction comes from a source accepted across political groups;
- the voter’s prior preference is weak or genuinely undecided;
- the correction arrives before attitudes have become firmly organised;
- repeated, consistent evidence prevents the claim from regaining familiarity;
- the electoral alternatives are close enough that the corrected fact can become decisive.

</div>

The source can be especially important. Adam Berinsky’s experiments on political rumours found that rebuttals did not work equally well merely because they presented the same facts; messengers who were politically unexpected or seen as credible could be more effective.[15] A correction from an apparent ally may be harder to dismiss as strategic opposition.

There is also evidence that positive information can be withdrawn successfully. Kevin Cobb, Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler found that when favourable but false information about a politician was discredited, evaluations could become less positive.[16] This is a useful counterweight to claims that political loyalties always immunise candidates against correction.

The studies showing attitudinal movement do not overturn the broader verdict. Instead, they identify conditions under which a corrected fact acquires enough weight to affect the larger judgement.

## Why stable support is not a backfire effect

A backfire effect has a demanding definition: compared with an otherwise similar uncorrected group, recipients of the correction must become more convinced of the false claim. Later large-scale experiments suggest that this outcome is uncommon.[17][18]

Suppose belief in a claim falls from 70 per cent to 50 per cent after correction, while support for the candidate remains at 45 per cent. The correction improved accuracy but did not alter candidate support. Describing that result as backfire would confuse two outcomes.

Even a decline in trust towards the fact-checker is not automatically belief backfire. It may be an undesirable side-effect worth measuring, but it does not establish that the false proposition gained credibility. Similarly, anger, counterarguing or continued social-media sharing can occur without increased belief.

Clear terminology prevents two symmetrical mistakes. One is to claim victory whenever a fact-check changes a survey answer, ignoring whether the improvement lasts or affects consequential reasoning. The other is to declare failure whenever voters do not abandon a politician, ignoring successful factual updating. Serious evaluation measures both.

## What the evidence cannot yet settle

Most research in this area uses online experiments, hypothetical vote questions or short-term follow-ups. These designs are valuable because random assignment can isolate the effect of a correction. They are less well suited to showing whether an effect persists through a campaign and changes an authenticated vote.

Actual elections also expose people to news coverage, advertising, interpersonal discussion and repeated versions of the original claim. A clean experiment may understate this competition. Conversely, a survey conducted immediately after treatment may overstate durability. Studies of COVID-19 fact-checking demonstrate that corrections can improve accuracy initially while effects fade over subsequent weeks.[19] Political corrections may face the same problem.

Another limitation is the selection of claims. Researchers often choose statements that can be corrected clearly and measured in a questionnaire. The political claims most likely to alter votes may instead involve developing scandals, uncertain responsibility or contested interpretations for which no short factual verdict is adequate.

Finally, a study may be statistically unable to detect a small electoral effect. Candidate attitudes are comparatively stable, and switching votes is rare. Demonstrating a modest change requires larger samples than demonstrating movement on a targeted belief question.

## What would change the assessment?

The present judgement would become stronger if more preregistered studies followed participants from exposure to a correction through an actual election, measured repeated encounters with the claim and—where consent and electoral law permit—used validated turnout or other behavioural records. The most informative studies would test the entire causal sequence, not just beliefs at one moment.

Evidence would shift towards a larger electoral role for fact-checking if such studies consistently showed that corrected beliefs mediated durable changes in candidate evaluations and vote choice. Conversely, the case for a sharp belief-vote divide would strengthen if factual gains persisted while well-powered field studies repeatedly found no consequential movement even for salient claims among undecided voters.

For readers assessing a fact-checking claim now, four questions are more useful than asking simply whether it “worked”:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Did the correction reduce belief in the specific falsehood relative to a control group?
2. Did that improvement last after the immediate test?
3. Did it change candidate evaluations, policy preferences or behaviour?
4. Was the study large and realistic enough to detect those downstream changes?

</div>

Keeping those outcomes separate leads to the most defensible conclusion. Fact-checks can improve the informational quality of democratic choice without dictating the choice itself. A citizen who changes their mind about a fact but not their vote has not necessarily rejected reality; they may simply be making a broader judgement than the correction addressed.
