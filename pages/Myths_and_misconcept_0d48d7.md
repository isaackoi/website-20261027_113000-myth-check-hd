## Introduction

The evidence offers two important corrections to popular thinking about false beliefs. First, people are neither infinitely gullible nor immune to error: plausibility, repetition, emotion, prior knowledge and trust in the source all affect what seems true. Secondly, [debunking]({{ 'debunking/' | relative_url }}) generally works. Corrections can leave traces of the original claim behind, but the much-publicised idea that fact-checking routinely makes people cling more fiercely to falsehoods is itself misleading.[3][4] The practical lesson is to replace a myth with a clear, memorable explanation—not merely to label it wrong.

## What counts as a myth or misconception?

The word “myth” has several meanings. It can describe a traditional story that conveys cultural meaning without functioning as literal history. It can also mean a popular but unsupported factual claim, such as “humans use only ten per cent of their brains”. Calling a cultural narrative a myth therefore does not automatically mean that it is worthless or deceptive. Confusion arises when these different meanings are treated as interchangeable.

A misconception is narrower: it is a mistaken belief or mental model. Someone who thinks antibiotics kill viruses has misunderstood how the drugs work. A rumour, by contrast, is an unverified claim circulating between people. “Misinformation” normally means false or misleading information shared without regard to the sender’s intention, while “disinformation” refers to material deliberately created or distributed to deceive.[2][5] “Malinformation” is sometimes used for genuine information deployed misleadingly or maliciously, such as material stripped of context.

These categories describe information, not permanent types of person. The same individual may carefully check a medical claim yet accept a flattering political statistic without scrutiny. Moreover, an inaccurate statement is not necessarily a lie: lying requires knowledge and intent that cannot be inferred from falsity alone.

## Why false ideas can feel true

People cannot investigate every proposition from first principles. Everyday judgement therefore relies on useful shortcuts: Does the claim fit existing knowledge? Is it easy to understand? Have several apparently independent sources repeated it? Does it come from someone trusted? These shortcuts usually make communication possible, but they can also make a weak claim feel familiar and credible.

### Repetition creates familiarity, not certainty

Experiments have repeatedly found an “illusory truth effect”: previously encountered statements tend to receive higher truth ratings than unfamiliar ones.[6][7] The effect can occur even when people possess relevant knowledge, because familiarity makes a statement easier to process.[8] That does not mean repetition hypnotises an audience or guarantees belief. Warnings, attentive reasoning, implausibility and strong contrary knowledge can limit its influence.[9]

This distinction matters online. A reader may encounter the same assertion in a headline, a repost, a search snippet and a family message. What feels like confirmation by four sources may be one unsupported claim copied four times. Repetition is therefore a reason to locate the original evidence, not evidence in itself.

### Memory keeps the claim more easily than its status

Human memory does not preserve a perfect recording with a permanent “true” or “false” label attached. People may remember the substance of a claim while forgetting that it came from satire, an unreliable source or a subsequent correction. Researchers call the lingering influence of retracted information the “continued influence effect”.[1][10]

A classic experimental pattern involves an unfolding report—for example, a warehouse fire initially attributed to flammable materials. Even after that explanation is explicitly withdrawn, participants may use it when reasoning about the event if no satisfactory alternative is supplied.[11] Retraction removes an explanation but can leave a gap in the story. A replacement account helps because it gives memory something coherent to use instead.

### Emotion and identity influence attention

Emotionally charged information receives attention and is more likely to be passed on. In one large analysis of Twitter data, messages containing moral-emotional language spread more widely within political networks.[12] That finding does not prove that every emotional claim is false; accurate warnings and justified anger can also be emotionally powerful. It shows why vivid anecdotes often outcompete cautious explanations.

Identity matters in a similarly qualified way. People may scrutinise information that threatens their group more harshly than information that flatters it. Yet research also shows that simple inattention and failure to consider accuracy explain a substantial share of low-quality sharing.[13] Not every mistaken post is an ideological declaration.

## Eight myths about misinformation

### 1. “People believe almost everything they see online”

People are often sceptical, especially towards unfamiliar sources. Exposure to fabricated news is also highly uneven. Research on the 2016 United States election found that consumption of identified false-news websites was concentrated among a relatively small segment of the population rather than evenly distributed among all internet users.[14][15]

This is reassuring but not grounds for complacency. A claim need not persuade everyone to cause harm; it may only need to reach the right community at a vulnerable moment. The more accurate picture is selective credulity: people reject much of what they encounter but can accept claims that seem plausible, familiar, socially endorsed or useful.

### 2. “False stories always spread faster than truth”

A prominent study of about 126,000 stories circulated on Twitter between 2006 and 2017 found that false news travelled farther, faster and more broadly than true news in that dataset, with novelty and human sharing playing important roles.[16] The study is significant, but its result is often overgeneralised.

It does not establish an iron law covering every platform, language, subject or definition of falsehood. Platform design, recommendation systems, breaking-news conditions and the availability of authoritative information all affect diffusion. “Falsehood always wins” is therefore another myth; the defensible conclusion is that some digital environments reward novelty in ways that can advantage false claims.

### 3. “Only unintelligent or uneducated people fall for myths”

Education and analytical reasoning can improve discernment, but neither confers immunity. Experts can reason poorly outside their speciality, while intelligent people may become skilled at constructing arguments for conclusions they already prefer. Familiarity, time pressure, identity and misplaced trust affect highly educated people too.[17][18]

The stereotype is also counterproductive. Treating correction as a contest between the clever and the foolish makes error socially costly to admit. A better approach treats revising a belief as normal evidence of learning.

### 4. “A correction merely repeats and strengthens the myth”

Older guidance sometimes warned communicators never to repeat a false claim because repetition might embed it in memory. Repetition can indeed create familiarity, so careless amplification is a real risk. But evidence does not support a blanket prohibition. Corrections must often identify the claim clearly enough for readers to recognise what is being corrected.[3][19]

Effective formats lead with the accurate account, mention the false claim once and conspicuously label it false, explain why it fails, and finish by reinforcing the accurate account.[3] The danger comes from making the myth more vivid than the correction, not from naming it at all.

### 5. “Corrections usually backfire”

The “backfire effect” is the claim that contradiction commonly causes people—particularly political partisans—to strengthen their original belief. An influential early study reported examples under particular conditions.[20] Later experiments across many issues found backfire effects to be uncommon rather than routine.[4][21]

Corrections can still fail, be forgotten or leave residual influence. People may also update their factual belief without changing their political preference or behaviour. Those outcomes should not be confused with becoming more misinformed. The balance of evidence supports correcting important falsehoods, while testing wording and avoiding humiliation or partisan point-scoring.

### 6. “Just present the facts”

A bare verdict leaves readers with unanswered questions: What happened instead? Why did the original claim appear convincing? Which evidence is decisive? Corrections are generally stronger when they provide a causal alternative, expose the misleading technique and come from a source the audience can recognise as competent.[1][22][23]

Facts also need intelligible presentation. A technically accurate page that buries its conclusion beneath qualifications may lose to a false claim expressed in one memorable sentence. Clarity is not the enemy of nuance; it is what makes nuance usable.

### 7. “Fact-checking erases the false belief permanently”

Corrections usually reduce belief, but memory changes over time. The original claim may remain more familiar than the correction, especially if it continues to circulate. Meta-analyses find that debunking is effective overall while also identifying conditions that affect its durability, including the strength of the initial message and the detail of the corrective explanation.[22][23]

This makes correction a continuing communication task. Repeating the accurate explanation, updating old pages, linking corrections to the original material and ensuring that search results surface reliable accounts can matter as much as publishing a single fact-check.

### 8. “Technology alone can solve the problem”

Automated detection, warning labels and moderation can reduce exposure, but misinformation is not just a defective-content problem. It also involves distrust, unequal access to reliable information, political incentives, social belonging and genuine uncertainty. During an unfolding emergency, yesterday’s responsible provisional statement may need revision as evidence changes.

Technical systems also face trade-offs. Aggressive removal can catch satire, legitimate disagreement or evolving scientific claims; minimal intervention can allow demonstrably false material to flourish. The World Health Organization’s concept of “infodemic management” accordingly includes listening to public concerns, communicating uncertainty and strengthening access to trustworthy guidance—not merely deleting posts.[5][24]

## Familiar examples and what they teach

The “ten per cent of the brain” claim survives because it is short, hopeful and compatible with stories about hidden potential. Neuroscience does not suggest that ninety per cent of a healthy brain sits unused: imaging, lesion studies and the metabolic cost of brain tissue all show functions distributed across many regions, although not every neuron is maximally active at every moment.[25] The corrective explanation is more useful than the verdict because it distinguishes “not all areas peak simultaneously” from “most of the brain has no function”.

The belief that antibiotics treat colds and influenza has more immediate consequences. Antibiotics target bacteria, not the viruses responsible for those illnesses. Unnecessary use can cause side effects and encourage antimicrobial resistance.[26][27] Here the strongest correction pairs a prohibition with an alternative: explain which symptoms require medical attention, how viral illness is managed and when bacterial complications might justify antibiotics.

The supposed link between the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine and autism demonstrates how a dramatic claim can outlive its evidential basis. The original 1998 paper was retracted, and large subsequent studies have not found an increased autism risk after vaccination.[28][29][30] Simply saying “the study was wrong” leaves the public story incomplete. A responsible explanation also distinguishes temporal sequence from causation, describes the later population evidence and acknowledges that autistic people and their families deserve support without being used as rhetorical devices in a vaccine dispute.

Historical misconceptions show a different problem. The notion that educated medieval Europeans universally believed in a flat Earth is contradicted by historical scholarship documenting knowledge of the Earth’s spherical shape.[31] Such myths often persist because they offer an appealing moral tale—in this case, enlightened modernity defeating ignorant predecessors. Correcting them requires replacing the caricature with a more interesting account of what people actually debated.

## How to assess a doubtful claim

No quick checklist can determine truth automatically, but a disciplined sequence can prevent the most common errors.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Identify the exact claim.** Separate measurable assertions from opinion, prediction, metaphor and moral judgement. “This treatment cured me” reports an experience; “this treatment cures the disease” makes a general causal claim requiring stronger evidence.

2. **Trace it to the origin.** Search for the study, recording, court document, dataset or official statement rather than relying on a screenshot or summary. Check whether several reports lead back to the same source.

3. **Read laterally.** Skilled fact-checkers leave an unfamiliar webpage and investigate its publisher, author and claims in other sources rather than judging credibility from professional appearance alone.[32][33]

4. **Inspect the evidence in context.** Ask whether a study involved humans or animals, whether its sample was large enough, whether it was observational or experimental, and whether other researchers reproduced the result. For statistics, examine the denominator, comparison group and time period.

5. **Look for the strongest contrary case.** A claim that survives only because dissenting evidence is ignored has not been seriously tested. Equally, the existence of disagreement does not mean both positions have equal evidential support.

6. **Check the date and current status.** Accurate photographs, figures and quotations become misinformation when attached to the wrong event. Scientific and official guidance can also change legitimately as evidence accumulates.

7. **Pause before sharing.** Brief prompts to consider accuracy have improved the quality of information people say they would share in experimental and field settings.[34] The pause is modest, but it interrupts the shift from “Is this true?” to “Will my group like this?”

</div>

## How to correct a myth without amplifying it

A useful correction is brief enough to remember but complete enough to replace the faulty account. Evidence-based debunking guidance recommends a four-part structure:[3]


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

- **Lead with the fact.** State the accurate conclusion in plain language.
- **Warn once about the myth.** Signal clearly that a false or misleading claim is about to be mentioned.
- **Explain the error.** Identify the missing context, impossible mechanism, unreliable evidence or deceptive technique.
- **Restate the fact.** End with the account readers should retain.

</div>

Tone matters alongside structure. Public shaming can turn a factual exchange into a threat to status or identity. Correct the claim rather than diagnosing the person, and acknowledge legitimate uncertainty where it exists. “The available studies have not shown a benefit” is different from “science has proved this can never work”.

Timing matters too. “Prebunking” or inoculation exposes people to a weakened example of a misleading technique—such as false dilemmas, impersonation or emotionally manipulative language—before they encounter it in the wild. Studies using games and short videos suggest that this can improve recognition of manipulation, although effects vary and may fade, so it should complement rather than replace accurate information and responsive correction.[35][36][37]

## The central misconception

The deepest misconception is that myths are alien intrusions into otherwise perfect reasoning. In reality, they exploit the same systems that make human thought efficient: trust, storytelling, pattern recognition, social learning and memory. The aim cannot be to eliminate every error or to make people distrust everything. Chronic cynicism is not the same as critical thinking.

A healthier information culture makes good evidence easier to find, uncertainty easier to understand and correction less humiliating. It rewards people who change their minds, distinguishes honest mistakes from organised deception and recognises that truth is more than a label attached to content. It is a practice of checking origins, comparing explanations and remaining willing to revise a conclusion when the evidence warrants it.
