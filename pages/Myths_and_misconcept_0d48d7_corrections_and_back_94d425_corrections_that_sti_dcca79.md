## Introduction

A correction is most likely to stick when it gives the accurate account a clear advantage in attention, explanation and memory. Start with the truth, identify the false claim only as precisely as necessary, explain what is wrong with it, and replace it with a coherent alternative. Then reinforce the accurate account through a trusted channel.[1][2]

The shortest usable structure is: **fact → warning → myth → explanation → fact**. It is sometimes called a “truth sandwich”, although the label is a practical communication aid rather than a scientifically unique formula.[3] Research supports its main components: prominent facts, explicit falsehood labels, explanatory rebuttals and credible alternatives generally outperform vague denial.[4][5] No format guarantees permanent belief change. Corrections can fade, and the original claim may continue to shape reasoning. The aim is therefore not merely to say that a myth is wrong, but to make the accurate explanation easier to understand, retrieve and repeat.

## Build the correction in seven steps

### 1. Verify the correction before publishing it

A correction loses value if it overstates the evidence, answers a different claim or requires its own correction later. Begin by reducing the disputed claim to a proposition that can actually be checked.

Ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- What, exactly, is alleged to have happened?
- Which part is demonstrably false, misleading or unsupported?
- What evidence would establish that judgement?
- Is the evidence current enough for the claim’s time frame?
- Which details remain uncertain?

</div>

This prevents a common error: rebutting an exaggerated version of the myth while leaving its real appeal untouched. If a circulating post says a medicine “always causes” a particular condition, evidence that the condition is uncommon addresses the universal claim. It does not prove that the event has never occurred. Precise correction is more credible than replacing one absolute with another.

Distinguish fact from interpretation as well. A data source may establish that a rate fell during a particular period without establishing why it fell. A strong correction marks that boundary instead of using accurate statistics to imply an unsupported causal story.

### 2. Lead with the accurate account

Put the fact in the headline, opening sentence or first visible panel. Readers should be able to understand the correction’s position without first absorbing a vivid restatement of the myth.

Compare:

- Weak: “Did the new vaccine alter recipients’ DNA?”
- Stronger: “The vaccine does not alter a recipient’s DNA.”

The second version reduces ambiguity and gives the accurate proposition the most prominent position. This matters online, where people may see only a headline, preview or screenshot.

Leading with the truth does not mean hiding the myth completely. Readers need enough information to connect the correction with the claim they encountered. The practical goal is prominence, not avoidance: make the accurate account the organising idea and the myth a clearly labelled object of correction.[3][6]

### 3. Warn before naming the myth

Introduce the false claim with an unmistakable cue: “A false post claims…”, “This figure is misleading because…”, or “There is no evidence for the allegation that…”.

The warning helps bind the claim to its status. It also reduces the risk that someone skimming the page will mistake a repeated falsehood for the correction itself. Typography, captions and social-media cards should preserve that relationship; a large myth quotation followed by a small correction can reverse the intended hierarchy.

Use the myth’s essential wording once if recognition requires it. Avoid repeating dramatic slogans, hashtags or memorable false statistics throughout the piece merely for rhetorical effect. Repetition can increase familiarity, and familiarity can later be mistaken for truth, especially when people forget where they encountered the statement.[7][8] Yet this risk should not be exaggerated: clearly labelled repetition within an effective correction normally improves accuracy rather than producing routine backfire.[9]

### 4. Diagnose the error, not just the verdict

“False” is a conclusion. A useful correction shows how that conclusion was reached.

The explanation might reveal that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- a photograph comes from a different event;
- a chart begins at a misleading date;
- two percentages use different denominators;
- an observational association has been presented as proof of causation;
- a quotation omits the sentence that changes its meaning;
- an authentic document has been given an invented description;
- a real adverse event was reported, but reporting did not establish that the product caused it.

</div>

Meta-analytic evidence indicates that detailed counter-messages tend to be more effective than bare negation.[4] Explaining the error gives readers a reason to revise their belief and a way to recognise the same tactic when the claim reappears.

Keep the diagnostic step proportional. A long technical detour can make the correction harder to remember than the myth. State the decisive flaw first; supporting detail can follow for readers who want to inspect it.

### 5. Fill the explanatory gap

A myth often survives because it supplies a complete story. A retraction removes a detail without replacing the role it played.

Classic continued-influence experiments used reports about a warehouse fire. Participants initially learnt that volatile materials had been found in a cupboard and were later told that this information was incorrect. Even people who remembered the retraction continued to refer to the materials when explaining the fire.[10] The false detail had provided a cause; after its removal, the story contained a hole.

Corrections become more durable when they supply a plausible replacement. Experiments have found that additional explanations can reduce later reliance on retracted misinformation more effectively than denial alone.[11][12] A useful alternative need not explain everything, but it should answer the question the myth appeared to answer.

For example:

> **Fact:** The surge in reported cases followed a change in testing, not a sudden change in the population’s underlying risk.
> **False claim:** Posts wrongly describe the higher count as proof that the condition suddenly became more common.
> **Explanation:** The new testing programme identified cases that previously went unrecorded. Rates calculated using a consistent method did not show the claimed jump.
> **Fact:** The reporting system changed; the available evidence does not show a sudden rise in underlying risk.

The alternative must itself be supported. Inventing a neat explanation merely to compete with the myth substitutes one unsupported story for another. Where the cause is unknown, say so and explain what has been ruled out, what remains possible and what evidence would resolve the question.

### 6. Show the evidence and its provenance

Readers should be able to see why the source is in a position to know. Name the relevant dataset, original document, investigation, image archive or specialist body. Link to primary material where practical, and distinguish a source’s expertise from its institutional prestige.

Source credibility can affect correction, but its influence is not mechanically transferable. A trusted doctor may be persuasive about clinical evidence but not election administration. A statistical agency may be authoritative about a published series but not about the motives behind a policy. Research on political rumours and social-media health misinformation suggests that recognised experts and unexpected or cross-cutting messengers can improve correction in some settings.[13][14] Other studies find that the correction’s substance can matter more than the label attached to its source.[1]

A robust correction therefore does not say only “experts disagree”. It makes the evidential path visible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. identify the source;
2. state what it measured or examined;
3. describe the finding relevant to the claim;
4. acknowledge material limitations;
5. provide access to the underlying evidence.

</div>

This transparency also makes the correction more portable. Someone repeating it in another setting can preserve the reasoning rather than relying on a naked appeal to authority.

### 7. Reinforce the accurate account

End with the fact in plain language. The final line should work even if separated from the rest of the correction.

Repetition is useful when it strengthens the accurate account rather than repeatedly advertising the myth. Studies of misinformation and correction show that reminders can reduce continued influence, although no reminder permanently immunises memory against later exposure.[15] In fast-moving situations, update the correction when new evidence appears and repeat it at the point where people are likely to encounter the false claim again.

The repeated version need not be identical. A headline, chart annotation, short social post and detailed article can all express the same core fact for different settings. Consistency matters more than word-for-word duplication.

## Why retractions alone leave a gap

Human memory does not simply replace an old statement with a new one. It can preserve the original claim, its context, the correction and later inferences as linked but competing information. Under distraction or time pressure, the older or more vivid explanation may be retrieved first.[16][17]

This is the continued-influence effect. It differs from the much rarer backfire effect: people may believe the myth less after correction yet still use part of it when explaining an event. Explicit warnings, repeated retractions and strong encoding of the correction reduce this influence, but may not eliminate it.[18]

The practical lesson is that a correction should repair a mental model, not merely attach a “false” label. Four questions expose an explanatory gap:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

- What did the myth appear to explain?
- Which causal link does the correction remove?
- What should replace that link?
- Can the replacement be stated more simply than the original claim?

</div>

Suppose a viral post blames a city-wide mobile-phone outage on a cyberattack. “There was no cyberattack” removes the alleged cause but leaves the outage unexplained. “Engineers traced the outage to a failed power component at the main switching centre; network logs show no intrusion” both rejects and replaces the claim.

This does not require a satisfying answer in every case. “Investigators have not yet established the cause” is preferable to speculation. It becomes more useful when paired with confirmed facts: what investigators checked, what evidence contradicts the rumour and when further findings are expected.

## Use a truth-first structure without treating it as magic

The truth-sandwich format is popular because it captures several sensible principles in a memorable sequence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Fact:** state the accurate account.
2. **Warning:** signal that misinformation follows.
3. **Myth:** identify the false claim briefly.
4. **Fallacy or explanation:** show why it is wrong and supply the better account.
5. **Fact:** restate the accurate takeaway.

</div>

The format aligns with research on refutation texts, explanatory alternatives and the clear labelling of misinformation.[3][19] However, evidence for the ingredients is stronger than evidence that this exact five-part order always outperforms every alternative. Message length, prior belief, subject matter and delivery channel can change what works.

For a simple numerical error, the correction may need only three sentences. For a conspiracy narrative connecting many events, one truth sandwich may be too small; the communicator may need to separate the narrative into checkable claims. For an unfolding emergency, the most honest structure may be “known, unknown, next update” rather than a complete alternative explanation.

The test is functional: can a reader identify the true account, understand the error and recall the replacement later? Following a template while burying the evidence does not meet that test.

## Choose timing for the situation

A correction can work after exposure; it is not automatically “too late”. Research comparing warning times indicates that both advance warnings and post-exposure corrections can improve accuracy, while their relative performance depends on what people must remember and when they are tested.[20]

Use timing deliberately:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

- **Before predictable misinformation:** warn people about a likely false claim or manipulation tactic, then give the accurate account. This is useful before elections, public-health campaigns and recurring seasonal rumours.
- **During rapid circulation:** attach the correction as close as possible to the claim, so readers do not encounter the myth without its status.
- **After exposure:** correct promptly, but include enough detail to reconnect the person’s memory of the claim with the retraction.
- **At later intervals:** repeat the accurate account when the issue returns to public attention.

</div>

Do not delay a well-supported correction solely because the ideal wording is unfinished. A short initial notice can state what is false and what is known, followed by a fuller explanation. Equally, speed does not justify certainty beyond the evidence. A correction that later collapses can damage trust in subsequent messages.

## Match the messenger to the audience

The best messenger is not always the most senior institution. It is the source with relevant knowledge, access to evidence and enough trust to be heard by the intended audience.

For a manipulated weather photograph, the original photographer or image archive may be decisive. For a false medicine claim, a regulator can explain the approval record while a clinician translates the practical implications. For a local election rumour, the official responsible for the relevant procedure can describe the safeguards and records.

Corrections from within a person’s own community may reduce the suspicion that rebuttal is an attack from an opposing group. Cross-partisan messengers can be useful when their position is genuinely unexpected, although the effect varies and should not be assumed.[13] On social media, direct correction by another user can improve accuracy, but expert organisational sources often provide stronger authority for specialised health claims.[14][21]

Trust should not be manufactured through false neutrality. Hiding sponsorship, using invented grassroots voices or overstating consensus may create a short-term persuasive advantage at the cost of credibility. State who is speaking, what evidence they possess and where their knowledge ends.

## Design for the place where the myth appears

A technically excellent correction can fail if its accurate content disappears when shared.

For social-media posts, put the fact in the first visible lines and ensure the image or preview card also signals the correction. Avoid thumbnails that display only the myth. Link to a fuller explanation, but do not require a click before the reader learns the central fact.

For spoken correction, keep the core sentence short and repeat it after the explanation. In a classroom, ask learners to explain why the misconception fails and apply the corrected model to a new example. Refutation-text research suggests that active integration of the new explanation can support knowledge revision.[19]

For charts, label the disputed comparison directly. If a misleading graph uses a truncated time window, show the longer series and explain why the missing period changes the conclusion. Visual correction should reveal the error rather than rely on decorative warnings.

For search results, use a title that supplies the answer. A correction headed only “The truth about…” leaves both readers and search systems uncertain about its conclusion.

## Common failures and how to repair them

A correction that does not work may have failed at a particular stage rather than triggered a mysterious backfire.

**The myth is remembered but the verdict is forgotten.**
Reduce repetition of the false claim, strengthen the warning, and make the accurate account the headline and final line. Familiarity can outlast memory for contextual labels.[7][22]

**Readers accept the correction but repeat the same explanation.**
Supply a causal alternative. Check whether the correction answers the “why” question that the myth appeared to settle.[10][11]

**The source is rejected immediately.**
Add primary evidence, explain the source’s relevant expertise, and consider a credible messenger recognised by the audience. Do not merely add more institutional logos.

**The correction is accurate but incomprehensible.**
State the conclusion first, define the decisive evidence and move technical detail into a second layer. Short-format fact-checks can be effective when they retain a clear explanatory rebuttal.[23]

**The effect fades.**
Repeat the fact at meaningful intervals and place reminders where the myth is likely to recur. Research on political and public-health corrections shows that accuracy gains can diminish over time.[22][24]

**Knowledge improves but behaviour does not.**
Do not automatically rewrite the factual message. Behaviour may depend on cost, convenience, identity, social norms or competing priorities. Measure belief, confidence, intention and action separately.

**The correction sounds more certain than the evidence.**
Replace absolute language with calibrated wording. “No credible evidence currently supports the claim” is stronger, not weaker, when that is what the record establishes.

## Test whether the correction really stuck

Immediate agreement is only the first outcome. A practical evaluation should test whether readers can use the correction after the original message is no longer in view.

Useful measures include:

- recognition of the accurate claim;
- recall of why the myth is wrong;
- ability to explain the alternative account;
- confidence calibrated to the evidence;
- resistance after seeing the myth again;
- correct application to a related example;
- relevant behaviour, where the correction was intended to guide action.

Use an uncorrected comparison group where possible. Without one, a communicator cannot tell whether residual belief reflects failure, improvement from a worse baseline or unrelated change. A genuine backfire finding also requires this comparison: it is not enough that some people remain mistaken after reading the correction.[25]

Delayed tests are especially valuable. A message that performs well after five minutes may leave little trace after a week. Research on corrections finds both durable improvements and decay, depending on repetition, prior belief and later exposure.[22][24] For high-stakes recurring myths, monitoring should continue after publication rather than ending with click-through rates or favourable comments.

## A compact working template

For most public corrections, the following draft is a strong starting point:

> **Accurate account:** State the fact in one sentence.
> **Warning:** Say that a specific circulating claim is false or misleading.
> **Claim:** Identify it briefly enough for recognition.
> **Diagnosis:** Explain the decisive mistake or missing context.
> **Replacement:** Give the supported explanation, including uncertainty where necessary.
> **Evidence:** Name and link the primary source.
> **Takeaway:** Repeat the fact in language that stands alone.

Before publishing, check that the accurate statement is more prominent than the myth; the explanation fills rather than merely exposes a gap; the evidence supports every part of the replacement account; and the final takeaway survives cropping, quotation and reposting.

A durable correction is not the loudest contradiction. It is the message that leaves readers with a better account to think with after the false one has been rejected.
